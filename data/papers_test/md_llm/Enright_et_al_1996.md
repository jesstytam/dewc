## Summary

1 *Banksia hookeriana* is a serotinous nonsprouting shrub restricted to the fire-prone northern sandplain scrub-heaths of Western Australia. Evidence of the natural fire frequency to which the species is best adapted was sought through analysis of its seed demography using a computer model of canopy seed bank dynamics based on field data.

2 Plants began flowering at 3-4 years of age but storage of viable seeds in the canopy was rare for plants < 5 years. Infructescences (cones) contained a mean of 12 follicles, holding two seeds of which 62% were estimated to be viable.

3 Cone (and seed) production increased with plant age to 15 years, but then fluctuated widely around a mean of 17 cones per year. By this time the average plant had an estimated canopy seed bank of 700 viable seeds comprising seeds 1–10 years old.

4 Seeds were lost from the canopy seed bank through spontaneous follicle rupture (i.e. in the absence of fire), granivory by insect larvae, and decay. Most follicles remained closed for at least 5 years and up to 50% of follicles were still closed after 10 years. Insect granivores destroyed 18% of seeds in 1-year-old cones, and continued to destroy seeds in older cones at a rate of 2-3% per year.

5 The seed bank model predicted continued seed accumulation in the canopy to at least age 25 years (1400 seeds), but by this time more viable seeds (~ 2200) had been lost to spontaneous opening, granivory and decay than were then stored on the plant.

6 Shannon–Weiner diversity (H′) of seed ages in the canopy seed bank increased with plant age, while evenness (V′) decreased at first (to plant age 17 years) and then increased.

7 On a time-scale of many generations, the likelihood of successful recruitment of individuals in the next generation (on average), occurs for a fire frequency of 15-18 years. At this frequency resource wastage in this low-nutrient environment would be minimized.

8 Recent fires have recurred within 7-11 years and indicate a human impact on the natural fire regime which may ultimately threaten the species.

*Keywords*: *Banksia*, fire frequency, seed bank model, seed demography, serotiny

## Introduction

Serotiny refers to the retention of seeds in closed fruits within the plant canopy for more than one year, and can be contrasted with two alternative fates of seeds: storage in a soil seed bank, or release of short-lived seeds upon maturity (no seed storage) (Lamont *et al.* 1991a). Seed release in serotinous species is normally triggered by fire and seeds are short-lived after release, germinating during the first favourable period (Cowling & Lamont 1987). Species can range from weakly serotinous, where most seeds are released within a few years of production, to strongly serotinous, where seeds are held in the canopy for many years. The

ecological significance of serotiny seems clear: where conditions for seedling recruitment are best immediately following fire (i.e. competition for light, moisture and nutrients are probably at their minimum), cued release of canopy-stored seeds after fire maximizes the age, and therefore canopy seed store, of the population by the time of the next fire. Any seedlings that establish later during the interfire period have a low probability of survival due to competition with their parents and other established plants (Cowling & Lamont 1987; Lamont & Barker 1988). They will be much younger and, on average, will have a smaller canopy seed bank when fire recurs and so are less fit (*sensu* Stearns 1992).

The dynamics of seed production, storage and loss in relation to plant age determines the range of fire frequencies for which population maintenance is possible. If fire occurs either before the onset of reproductive maturity, or before a sufficient canopy seed bank has accumulated, then a population is threatened with extinction.

In the absence of fire some seeds are released by the spontaneous opening of old fruits, but the likelihood of plant recruitment and survival to reproductive age is extremely low. Seed release is hastened by the death of stem tissue due to drought stress and plant senescence (Witkowski *et al.* 1991). This means that the canopy seed bank may be completely lost where plant death occurs before the next fire, a fate not shared by most soil seed bank species where seeds may survive for many years after the death of parent plants (Baker 1989). Because dry plant biomass is more readily consumed by fire, any seeds held in the canopy of dead plants at the time of fire are also likely to perish (Lamont & Barker 1988).

*Banksia hookeriana* Meissn. (Proteaceae) is a highly serotinous, fire-killed (nonsprouting) woody shrub to 2.5 m high which is restricted to the northern sandplain scrub-heaths of south-western Australia about 250-330 km north of Perth (George 1981; Taylor & Hopper 1988). Seeds are held in a state of enforced dormancy within woody follicles on large infructescences (cones) in the crown. While new inflorescences may be damaged and removed from plants by birds, mature cones normally remain attached to the plant throughout its life. Some seeds abort during development, while others are either released gradually or (especially after fire) are consumed by insects, lose viability and decay, or remain viable. Once released seeds germinate with the onset of winter rains or perish as there is no persistent soil seed bank (Enright & Lamont 1989).

The geographical extent (80 × 30 km) of the low nutrient, noncalcareous, sandplain defines the distribution of this species, which is further limited by its specific preference for the deeper sands of low dune crests (Enright & Lamont 1989; Lamont *et al.* 1989). This means that the species exists as a large number of discrete populations of varying sizes (< 100 to > 10 000 individuals) separated by distances of 0.2 to > 1 km. The largest populations are located near Mt Adams in the northern part of the species range. Land clearance for agriculture, mining and mineral exploration, and increased incidence of anthropogenic ignition, make habitat loss and increased fire frequency potential threats to the long term survival of this species. In addition, its attractive orange inflorescences are harvested in large numbers for the commercial wildflower trade, so reducing seed production in exploited populations (Witkowski *et al.* 1994).

This paper describes seed production and availability, and a computer model of seed bank dynamics in *Banksia hookeriana* in relation to plant age (= time since last fire) for sites near Mt Adams. The following questions were posed:

1 What is the pattern of seed production with plant age, and what factors influence the rate of seed accumulation in the crown (e.g. level of serotiny, granivory, seed abortion and longevity)?

2 Does the analysis of seed demography reveal the optimum fire frequency to which the species is adapted, and the range of fire frequencies compatible with population survival?

### STUDY AREA

The study was located in the Yardanogo Nature Reserve about 330 km north of Perth (29°25′S, 114°58′E). Mean annual rainfall at the two closest climate stations is 513 mm (Eneabba; 55 km to the south) and 471 mm (Dongara; 25 km to the north), respectively. There is a pronounced winter maximum with almost no rain (median 20 mm) in the five summer months from December to April. Temperature regimes are similar for both climate stations: mean maximum temperature in the hottest month (January) is 35.8 °C, mean minimum temperature in the coolest month (July) is 9.2 °C. Soils vary from acidic, leached sands on low dunes to sandy clays in intervening depressions (Hnatiuk & Hopkins 1981). The vegetation of the low dunes is a diverse scrub-heath usually dominated by two *Banksia* species, *Banksia hookeriana* and *Banksia attenuata* R. Br., although up to six banksias may co-occur (Beard 1984; Enright & Lamont 1989).

## Materials and methods

Seven sample plots, each 400 m² in area and ranging in age since last fire from 1 to 3 and 14-16 years, were established in 1986 and monitored for 6-8 years. They were on the crests and slopes of low, sandy dunes in scrub-heath along the edge of a 2-km section of Mt Adams Road within the Yardanogo Nature Reserve. Sites covering a broader range of *Banksia hookeriana* stand ages could not be located.

For the five plots where plant age was 1-3 years at the start of the study, and when fire occurred subsequently during the study period (wildfire killed all plants in four of these five plots in 1991), age at time of last fire was estimated for 10 plants by the stem node ageing method described by Lamont (1985), or was known precisely. Seed production was estimated by counting the total number of cones on a random sample of 10 plants and the number of follicles on each of 10 randomly chosen cones per plant. The onset and level of flower head (inflorescence) and cone (infructescence) production were recorded within five replicate 25-m² subplots per plot for each year until 1991 (four plots) or 1993 (one plot).

For the two plots where plant age was 14-16 years in 1986, the total number of plants and total number of cones per plant was determined. For a random sample of 10 plants per plot, the height and crown diameter, and number of new cones was enumerated each year there-after until 1991 (when wildfire killed all plants in one plot) or 1993. Plots were searched each year for evidence of seedling establishment from spontaneous release of seeds in the absence of fire.

Complete cone crops were harvested from 10 randomly chosen 16-year-old plants at the start of the study in 1986. Cones were allocated to annual age classes using the stem node ageing method so that the entire history of reproduction could be determined for each plant. Degree of serotiny was based on the proportion of follicles per cone age class which had opened in the absence of fire (Cowling & Lamont 1985a). Cones were burned and subjected to wet-dry cycles to induce follicle rupture and facilitate seed removal (Cowling & Lamont 1985b). The numbers of follicles, and proportions of firm, aborted, eaten and decayed seeds were determined per plant by cone age. Viability of firm seeds was assessed by germinating batches of 25 seeds per cone age class from each of five plants (replicates) under moist conditions at 15 °C (Cowling *et al.* 1987).

### STATISTICAL ANALYSES

Trends between seed parameters, plant age and cone age were analysed using regression models (SYSTAT 1992). Where data were not normally distributed, logarithmic (ln) and arcsine transformations were employed to satisfy assumptions of the regression models used. All error terms listed are standard errors.

A canopy seed bank dynamics model, written in programming language C++, was used to estimate the pattern of viable seed retention on plants and fate of seeds in relation to age. The model used regression equations and other statistics relating patterns of seed accumulation and loss to increasing plant and cone age. Shannon–Weiner diversity (H′) and evenness (V′) were calculated for the distribution of viable seed ages by plant age.

## Results

### CONE PRODUCTION

A few plants produced inflorescences at 3-4 years of age, but these were all sterile (age 3: mean inflorescences per plant = 0.12 ± 0.06; age 4: mean = 0.92 ± 0.23). Plants first produced fertile cones at an age of 5 years. Cone production increased until plants were about 15 years old, after which annual production fluctuated widely about an average of 17 cones per year (Fig. 1a; Table 1). Both within and between year variability in cone production per plant increased with plant age.

The total number of cones held in the crown increased continuously with plant age and although the standard error also increased with age, the mean pattern of accumulation showed a consistent trend (Fig. 1b, Table 1).

### SEROTINY, GRANIVORY, DECAY AND SEED STORAGE

In the absence of fire nearly all follicles remained closed for the first 5 years. Thereafter the rate of spontaneous seed release increased linearly (Fig. 2c, Table 1). After 10–12 years the cones were heavily weathered and many of the seeds decomposed so that few viable seeds remained.

Since plants only began to set viable seed at an age of about 5 years, and little spontaneous follicle rupture occurred in cones less than 5 years old, there was almost no seed loss due to follicle rupture in plants up to 10 years old. Above this age, the percentage seed loss increased markedly: 5% of all follicles on plants were open by age 13 years, 10% by 16 years, and 20% by age 21 years. No seedlings which established in the sample plots as a result of interfire seed release survived for more than one year.

The decline in seed storage with cone age was also attributable to seed loss through granivory and decay (Table 1). Loss of seeds through fungal decay followed a similar pattern to that described for spontaneous seed release due to follicle rupture in the absence of fire. Decay was almost absent until cones were 6 years old, after which the proportion of seeds lost increased rapidly.

Granivory within closed follicles by the larvae of two weevils, *Cechides amoenus* (Curculionidae; Cleoninae) and *Eleale aulicodes* (Curculionidae; Cleridae), and a jewel beetle (Bupestridae; *Astraeus* sp.) was observed. Unlike follicle opening and decay, granivory was recorded for all cone ages (Table 1).

By the time plants were 25 years old, the combined impacts of follicle rupture in the absence of fire, and decay, resulted in little further net accumulation of viable seed in the plant canopy and more seeds were lost than were gained per year (Fig. 4). The continued increase in H′ after annual seed production had peaked (15 years) and the number of seed ages represented had stabilised (18 years), resulted from increasing evenness (V′) of seed abundance in each age class. Evenness was initially high (V′ = 0.95 at 6 years), declined as each year's seed crop increased (V′ = 0.80 at 17 years), then increased again as seed production stabilised (V′ = 0.88 at 25 years).

Despite large year to year variations in cone production, on average the total number of cones in the crown showed a steady increase with age. McMaster & Zedler (1981), Lamont *et al.* (1991a), and others hypothesized that canopy seed storage (serotiny) may dampen annual fluctuations in production, ensuring adequate seed supply following fire. The present results support this contention: seeds produced in years of high seed production were nearly all held on the plant for at least 5 years so that years of low seed production did not impact in the short term on the total canopy seed store.

Cowling *et al.* (1987) found that years of high seed production were associated with low rates of predispersal granivory by insects, implying satiation of granivores. ### SEED BANK DYNAMICS AND FIRE FREQUENCY

According to Gill (1981) plants are not adapted to fire *per se*, but to a particular fire regime. The number of viable seeds stored in the crown of serotinous plants may largely determine the number offspring left after a fire. The balance between the accumulation of viable seeds in the canopy seed bank as plant age increases, and the loss of viable seeds to spontaneous follicle opening, granivory and decay might appropriately describe the optimum life history strategy for a nonsprouting species in relation to fire frequency. The canopy seed bank dynamics model estimates these patterns of accumulation and loss.

Early in the reproductive life of individuals of *Banksia hookeriana* (5–8 years of age) few viable seeds are produced, but few are lost. The absolute number of seeds available for dispersal following fire is small and there is a low likelihood of successful parent replacement given the high rates of seed and seedling mortality typical of the first few years following fire (Enright & Lamont 1989, 1992a,b). Using data on seedling recruitment after fire in Little Desert National Park, Victoria, Gill & McMahon (1986) estimated that parent replacement in *Banksia ornata* populations was unlikely until plants held ≈ 100 viable seeds in the crown. This correlated with a plant age of about 16 years. Independent evidence on recruitment of *Banksia hookeriana* near Mt Adams following experimental fires in 1983 and a wildfire in 1984 indicated that between 45 and 90 seeds were released for each recruit still alive in 1990 at the age of 6 or 7 years (Enright & Lamont 1992b). If it is assumed that 100 seeds are required to ensure parent replacement following fire, then the minimum postfire interval for *Banksia hookeriana* plants would be at least 8 years.

The total number of viable seeds lost from the canopy during the interfire period increased rapidly with age for plants > 10 years old. Nevertheless, the excess of total viable seeds produced in the plant's lifetime over total losses peaked at age 15 years. As plants continued to age in the absence of fire, total losses approached and finally exceeded the number of viable seeds still in the crown. This was predicted to occur for the average plant by the age of 22 years.

Using the simplifying assumption that self-replacement, on average, required release of at least 100 viable seeds per parent after fire, an estimate of the finite rate of natural increase (λ) can be calculated for each plant age (= fire frequency) with the maximum value of λ assumed to reflect the optimum strategy (λ as a measure of fitness, *sensu* Stearns 1992). Populations of *Banksia hookeriana* near Mt Adams appear optimally adapted to recurrent fires every 16–17 years (Fig. 5). Fires at this frequency also maximized the number of viable seeds released after fire relative to the total investment of energy and nutrients expended on seed production. Although the size of the canopy seed bank continued to grow until plants were about 25 years old, predicted λ decreased as plant age increased beyond 17 years. In addition, increasingly more resources are wasted on seeds which perish during the interfire period through spontaneous release into a highly competitive environment where successful recruitment is unlikely, and through predispersal granivory and decay.

For estimates ranging from half (50) to twice (200) the hypothesized number of seeds required per parent (relative to that used above), the optimum fire frequency ranges from 15 to 18 years, respectively (Fig. 5), and so appears quite robust.

If stands remain unburned for more than 25 years, opportunities for interfire recruitment might increase due to the release of sites within stands resulting from death of old individuals. However, Bond (1980) documents the decline of serotinous Proteaceae populations in South African fynbos which have remained unburned for 30-65 years, and we have recorded no successful interfire recruitment to reproductive age in *Banksia hookeriana* for stands up to 21 years old. The lack of older sites precludes further direct study of this question. The wastage of scarce resources (i.e. seed loss) in plants older than 22 years might imply that, historically, sites rarely reach this age before fire recurs. In support of this contention, we have been unable to locate any sites containing plants older than 23 years (Enright & Lamont unpublished data). However, this may also reflect a recent increase in fire frequency due to human interference.

Diversity and evenness in the canopy seed store modelled here had not peaked by the estimated optimum return time for fire. Nevertheless, diversity (H′) rises only slowly once this optimum is passed and evenness is always high. One possible consequence of dormant seed banks is the presence of seeds produced in different years which might reflect different pollen parents and selective forces (Harper 1977), i.e. it increases genetic diversity of the seed pool. This might help to buffer the species against rapid evolutionary drift, and at the same time increase resilience of the species in the face of fluctuating environmental conditions. These advantages apply to short-lived species characterized by soil seed storage where parent plants represent different generations. The advantages for longer lived, cohort regenerator species such as *Banksia hookeriana* are less obvious, as all or most pollen will derive from neighbours of identical age which were recruited following the same disturbance and have experienced similar growing conditions. Instead, selfing would probably increase with age as flower head production per plant and death of neighbours increased, cancelling out increasing seed age diversity. Cowling (1987) argued that serotinous nonsprouting species (such as *Banksia hookeriana*) are prone to strong selection pressure, particularly at the seedling stage. He considered that the high number of discrete sexual generations, and fragmentation of populations through fire-mediated local extinction, implies a rapid evolutionary rate and may explain the high level of speciation in *Banksia* and other taxa with similar life histories.

The recent history of fire within the geographical range of *Banksia hookeriana* suggests shorter intervals between successive fires than we propose as optimal for species' maintenance. A wildfire in January 1984 burned 117 000 ha of the northern sandplain heath (van der Moezel *et al.* 1987), an area equivalent to nearly half of the total geographical range of the species. In the autumn of 1991 wildfire again covered part of the 1984 burn area near Mt Adams, a return period of only 7 years. This fire eliminated *Banksia hookeriana* from one of the plots enumerated here. Van der Moezel *et al.* (1987) comment that areas of sandplain heath vegetation older than 11 years are becoming increasingly scarce since the establishment of farms and roads in the area and a hazard-reduction burning policy for the remaining natural vegetation.
