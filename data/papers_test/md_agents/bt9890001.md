# **Secondary Succession in Post-fire Scrub Dominated by** *Acacia verticillata* **(L'Hérit.) Willd. at Wilsons Promontory, Victoria**

*David H. Ashton and Jennifer A. Chappill*

School of Botany, University of Melbourne, Parkville, Vic. 3052.

#### *Abstract*

Senescence and death of 30-year-old post-fire *Acacia verticillata* scrub in areas originally supporting sclerophyll eucalypt forest were studied over a 6-year period at Wilsons Promontory, Victoria. The potential for eucalypt regeneration was also examined. On drier sites, *A. verticillata* has been replaced by drought-resistant *Kunzea ambigua* while on wet sites it has been replaced by waterlogging-tolerant *Melaleuca ericifolia.* In mid slopes *A. verticillata* is regenerating from soil seed in gaps in the field layer of *Pteridium esculentum* and *Goodenia ovata* and forming a patchy, uneven-aged stand. The composition of the mid-slope community is determined by the relative resistance of species to browsing and grazing. The failure of eucalypts to reclaim the scrub areas at this critical time has been due to poor seed crops, seed harvesting by ants and intense selective browsing. Exclosure plots have demonstrated the importance of herbivory in modifying this vegetation and indicate potential means of reafforestation without the intervention of fire.

### **Introduction**

In the moist areas of Victoria, large areas of high forest have been converted to scrub of varying height, density and species composition by the fire regimes of the last century (Ashton 1981). Such dramatic changes have been ascribed to the fire sensitivity of the fast growing eucalypt overstorey and its relatively long non-flowering period. Thus, repeated fires at short intervals, even those of low intensity, will kill regeneration before seed has been produced. Alternatively, a similar result will be achieved if severe fires occur at a time of negligible seed storage in the crowns, or if severe drought or intense selective browsing removes or severely damages eucalypt regeneration.

The total area of potential sclerophyll forest on Wilsons Promontory (Fig. 1) is estimated to be 95 km<sup>2</sup> and includes a wide range of structure from dry to wet sclerophyll forest (Ashton 1981; Gill 1981). Much of the present forest is intermediate between these extremes and conforms to the 'damp sclerophyll' type as described by Opie *et al.* (1984). At Wilsons Promontory (Fig. 1) approximately 14% of the tall forests of *Eucalyptus obliqua* L'Hérit., *E. muelleriana* and *E. regnans*\* have been destroyed in the last 60 years by numerous fires.

Only 2·4% of the forested area currently consists of the tall open forest formation with tall and dense understoreys of shrubs, ferns and tree ferns. Of the forested areas converted to scrub, about 68% (9·0 km<sup>2</sup>) are dominated by *Acacia verticillata* in damp sclerophyll areas and 32% (4·3 km<sup>2</sup>) is dominated by *Pomaderris aspera, Bedfordia arborescens, Olearia argophylla* and *O. phlogopappa* in the wetter areas. #### **Materials and Methods**

#### *Vegetation Structure and Composition*

Floristic composition was assessed in 1983 by gathering data from 78 quadrats (10 by 2 m) along three transects from the edge of the Tidal River swamp to the *K. ambigua*-dominated scrub on the upper slopes of Mt Bishop. The pattern in these data was revealed with the aid of the computer-based hierarchical agglomerative classification program MACINF with presence and absence data (Lance and Williams 1967) and principal coordinates analysis (PCOA) of the data matrix produced by MACINF. The programs were accessed in the CSIRO TAXON package (Ross 1983).

Profiles were drawn in three representative areas to display the structure of the vegetation.

The relative areas occupied by light (<25%), medium (25-75%) and heavy (75-100%) cover classes of the field layer were assessed from intercepts on 16 lines 10 m long on the lower slopes of the degenerate scrub in 1983.

#### *Regeneration of Eucalypts in the Scrub*

An experimental plot 200 m<sup>2</sup> was chosen in a homogeneous area of typical degenerate scrub on the lower slopes (Fig. 3). It was divided into four 10 by 5 m subplots, two of which were fenced with 4 cm wire-mesh netting to a height of 1 m. The fences excluded wallabies and wombats but not possums. Seventy-two seedlings of each of the three eucalypt species which grow nearby, i. e. *E. obliqua* and *E. muelleriana*\* in the moister areas and *E. baxteri* in the drier areas, were planted in December 1979 in a random block design in three cover classes of vegetation: natural vegetation, vegetation clipped to 15 cm height in a radius of 15 cm around the seedlings and areas completely cleared of vegetation to a radius of 15 cm around the seedlings. At each spot the seedlings were assigned randomly at three points of a triangle, 10 cm apart. This resulted in 12 replications of each treatment and species combination. Seedlings were first measured on 31/12/1979 and the changes in height, growth and survival were recorded at approximately monthly intervals until mid September 1980, and then at approximately yearly intervals until 1986.

Additional plantings were made of *E. obliqua* seedlings 1-1·5 m tall in the dense vegetation surrounding the experimental plots to determine whether larger plants would be better able to establish in the scrub. Seeds of the three eucalypts were sown separately in 10 small microsites of disturbed soil 30 cm in diameter outside the plots, to evaluate the level of predation by insects or mammals on very young stages and the success of germination. All seed was lightly covered by soil, as it had been shown by A. N. Andersen (personal communication) in nearby sites that insect predation by, for example, the ants *Rhytidoponera victoreae* Andre and *Chelaner sculpturatus* Clark. could account for losses of 33-83% of the seed placed on the soil surface within 2 weeks.

#### **Results**

#### *Vegetation Structure and Composition*

The classification of the floristic composition of quadrats (10 by 2 m) produced six groups (Fig. 4a) which could be interpreted in relation to topography. Although some groups (I, V) comprised only quadrats from scrub vegetation (Table 1), others (II) were equally distributed between scrub and forest or indicated a distinct preference for one or the other (VI, III). The greatest number of species occurred in forested lower-mid slopes (I), the lowest in wet scrub (IV). Most groups were characterised by gradations of species frequencies although the predominant community in the mid-lower slopes of *Melaleuca-Kunzea-Banksia-Acacia* was distinguished by the high frequency of *Adiantum aethiopicum* in the *Pteridium-Goodenia* field layer. The first two axes of the ordination of the groups (Fig. 4b) accounts for 60% of the quadrat variation. Axis 1 appears to be related loosely to topographic factors such as soil moisture gradients.

#### *Regeneration of* A. verticillata *and Other Species in the Scrub*

The development of the ground stratum in response to the exclusion of the larger herbivores from the fenced areas was dramatic (Table 2). The most conspicuous effect in the first 2 years was the increase in cover and height of the scrambling grass, *T. juncea* and the raspwort, *Gonocarpus teucrioides.* Both of these species declined sharply during the drought year of 1982 and only the former recovered its prior vigour (Fig. 10). *P. esculentum* behaved similarly to *T. juncea,* but *G. ovata* declined steadily as its associates increased. The cover repetition of species sensitive to browsing tended to be higher in the fenced plots, although *Coprosma quadrifida* did not conform, due to its development of compact, intricate spiny branches under heavy browsing (Table 2). Species diversity was greater in the fenced plots (26 species) than the unfenced plots (20 species).

In 1986 the increasing cover of eucalypts in the fenced plots appears to have reduced the vigour of the field layer. In the unfenced plots *T. juncea* decreased markedly, probably as a result of wombat grazing. The field layer, therefore, became increasingly dominated by non-grazed *P. esculentum* and *G. ovata* and with scattered spiny bushes of *C. quadrifida* and *A. verticillata.* *K. ambigua* and *M. ericifolia* which are increasing in density in these areas are scarcely browsed, although occasionally killed by wood-boring insects on the lower stems.

The density of natural regeneration of the major woody species in the mid-slope area of the scrub in 1982 and 1983 is shown in Table 3. *M. ericifolia* is regenerating vigorously from root suckers and *A. verticillata* is also regenerating well from soil-stored seed. The later species flowered and set seed abundantly in 1984 and thus further replenishment of the soil seed bank is likely to have occurred in 1985. The reduction of height and density of *A. verticillata* in the presence of browsing was evident in 1986.

The observed germination of soil-stored seed in the field and in seed trays is given in Table 4. The number of seedlings germinating from the seed trays was greatest from the upper 1 cm sample, but the species composition was similar for each depth; therefore, the results were pooled and adjusted to show numbers of germinated seedlings per square metre of soil to a depth of 3 cm. Most species showed greater germination in the trays than in the field, with the exception of *Hydrocotyle hirta* and *V. hederacea.* Some species such as *Bulbine bulbosa, Calystegia marginata* and *Schoenus apogon* appeared in the trays but were not observed in the natural vegetation. The notable absence of *T. juncea,* may be due to heavy grazing preventing normal flowering, since this species is normally present as soil seed in wet forests in central Victoria (Ashwell 1981).

The species subject to grazing and browsing are indicated in Table 5. Scat analysis indicates that black-tailed wallabies and the common wombat are the major herbivores and differ in their relative abundance along the topographic gradient. This will affect the proportion of grazing and browsing in different sites. Brushtail possums were present, and may have been responsible for minor browsing of eucalypt seedlings in 1980 within the fenced plots. Hog deer *(Axis porcinus)* are present in the area but do not appear to be as important as the marsupials at this time.

It is clear that the species which are most successful in the regeneration in this area have means of resistance to grazing pressure. Thus, both *C. quadrifida* and *A. verticillata* are protected to some extent by spines, whilst *G. ovata,* *K. ambigua* and *M. ericifolia* appear to be unpalatable to the mammals in the area, although insect attack may occur. In many places it is evident that the greatest amount of damage occurred in the vicinity of animal runs and in more open spaces between the denser vegetation. Dicotyledonous herbs, such as *V. hederacea, Stellaria flaccida* and *G. teucrioides* were severely grazed, but *P. esculentum* and *G. ovata* were conspicuously undamaged, although minor insect attack was evident.

#### **Discussion**

Purdie and Slatyer (1976) have pointed out that a single model of secondary succession is unlikely to describe adequately a given sere, and this is true for the post-fire scrubs at Wilsons Promontory. The development of sclerophyll open forests is the product of site quality and fire regime (Gill 1975). In the scrub areas the normal post-fire succession has been altered by the removal of the *Eucalyptus* overstorey component in the last 100 years by a regime of frequent, severe fires associated with European settlement (King 1963).

ance and longevity of other dominants such as *B. marginata,* *C. quadrifida, K. ambigua* and *M. ericifolia.* The successfully regenerating species are either unpalatable or spiny at shoot maturity. The importance of the soil seed bank and the ability of species to avoid the sensitive seedling stage by vegetative spread is clearly seen in the potentially dense field and ground strata of this vegetation. In this area the scattered individuals of *A. verticillata* flowered after 5 years, thus restoring to some extent the soil seed bank of this species.

The mid-slope areas show the greatest diversity both structurally and floristically. With the demise of the *A. verticillata* stratum the high percentage of bare ground was subject to the effects of insolation, and regeneration of soil seed commenced. The patchy regeneration of *A. verticillata,* which commenced about 10 years ago, is continuing, but the area available is being diminished as the field layer of *P. esculentum* and *G. ovata* increases in area, density and height. Therefore, at this stage, *A. verticillata* has contracted its range to the mid slopes where the previously dense, even-aged stand is being replaced by a patchy, uneven-aged community, with an age span of more than 15 years. Future cycles of regeneration may further exaggerate this tendency and produce a regeneration mosaic, sens. Watt (1947), although severe fires in the next century may again restore an even-aged community from the soil seed bank over the whole topographic range.

Only under very rare instances do eucalypt seedlings survive without the intervention of fire. The experimental plots demonstrate conclusively the overwhelming importance of the herbivore factor in preventing the regeneration of *Eucalyptus* species under otherwise favourable conditions. Much seed is harvested by insects, and seedlings beyond the two-leaf stage are readily browsed. Over the first few years little discrimination occurred between the three contending species in the area - *E. obliqua, E. muelleriana* and *E. baxteri.* When fenced, the latter two species have survived better in the luxuriant ground stratum, possibly as a consequence of their longer period of horizontally disposed juvenile foliage. However, these data may be biased by the different seed sources for *E. obliqua* and *E. muelleriana* used.

In the event of fire, adequate seed storage in the eucalypt canopy is a prerequisite for regeneration, but even so seed dispersal is likely to be limited to much less than 4-5 tree heights from the source (Ashton 1986). Small areas of scrub, therefore, may be expected to be encroached by eucalypts after fire but this will be a rare event in non-fire periods. Large areas of scrub, therefore, are likely to remain an intractable problem, even if fires of suitable frequency and type occur at times coinciding with abundant eucalypt seed production.

The management of these areas for the ultimate aim of restoration of forest would require the removal or control of the damaging herbivore population, which would be an anathema to the policy of a National Park. The alternative of spot sowing or planting appropriate eucalypt species with fencing guards would provide focal points from which subsequent regeneration may occur under the right conditions of disturbance. However, any measures would be successful only in the long-term because of the magnitude of the area of forest that has been devastated during a century of uncontrolled fire regimes.
